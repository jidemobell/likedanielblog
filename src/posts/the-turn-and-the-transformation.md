Someone said it in a message and it would not leave me alone: *people confess their sins, but they do not repent.*

I have watched that sentence be true. I have watched it be true in other people, and I have watched it be true in me. So I went back to where the phrase actually comes from — a man in camel's hair, standing in the wilderness, shouting at religious people before Jesus had preached a single public sermon.

What I found was not a neat call to feel sorry. It was a chain: John's fire, the fruit that proves a turn, the will that cannot carry it alone, and a house that gets swept clean and left empty.

## The Fire Before the Fire

Start with the detail that makes John strange. He is preaching wrath and fire — "who warned you to flee from the coming wrath?" (Matt. 3:7), "every tree that does not produce good fruit is cut down and thrown into the fire" (Matt. 3:10) — and he is doing it *before* the One he is announcing has said anything at all. Acts 13:24 confirms the order: "Before Jesus began his work, John preached to all the people of Israel that they should turn from their sins and be baptized."

So where did John get fire? Not from imagination. He inherited it.

| The expectation John inherited | Where it was written |
|---|---|
| The Lord comes with fire to execute judgment | Isaiah 66:15–16 |
| The day is coming, burning like a furnace; the arrogant become stubble | Malachi 4:1 |
| God calls for judgment by fire | Amos 7:4 |
| The whole earth consumed in the fire of His jealousy | Zephaniah 1:18 |
| A fiery throne, and books opened | Daniel 7:9–10 |

By the first century that imagery had hardened into a specific hope: the Messiah would arrive as the agent of that fire — separating, winnowing, and burning up the chaff. John was not innovating. He was reading his own Scriptures and drawing the obvious conclusion: *the Judge is at the door, and He is carrying fire.*

Then look at what John actually says the Coming One will do:

> "He will baptize you with the Holy Spirit and fire. His winnowing fan is in His hand, and He will thoroughly clean out His threshing floor, and gather His wheat into the barn; but He will burn up the chaff with unquenchable fire." — Matthew 3:11–12

Notice the order. **Spirit first, then fire.** One baptism, two effects: what purifies the wheat consumes the chaff. That is the hinge of the whole New Testament, and John said it before he understood it.

| What John expected | What actually happened | Where it landed |
|---|---|---|
| Fire falls on the wicked at His arrival | Fire falls on the *disciples* at Pentecost | Acts 2:3 |
| The axe is already at the root (Matt. 3:10) | The branch that bears no fruit is taken away | John 15:2 |
| The chaff burned with unquenchable fire | The fire is real, and He came to bring people out of it | Matt. 25:41; Mark 9:43–48 |
| A Judge with a winnowing fan | The same Judge, on a throne, at the end | Matt. 16:27; Rev. 20:11–15 |

John was not wrong. He was early. What changed between Malachi and Matthew is not the fire — it is the door. The Spirit is poured out *before* the fire falls, and that gap is the whole reason there is time to repent.

So John announced that the fire was coming. Jesus spent His ministry telling people how to be inside the house when it does.

## The Verse

> "Produce fruit in keeping with repentance." — Matthew 3:8

Two words carry it. *Metanoia* — repentance — is not remorse. It is a change of mind that issues in a change of direction. *Karpos* — fruit — is not intention. It is the observable yield of a life.

The Amplified Bible makes the demand uncomfortably concrete:

> "So produce fruit that is consistent with repentance [demonstrating new behavior that proves a change of heart, and a conscious decision to turn away from sin]."

*New behavior that proves a change of heart.* John is not asking for tears. He is asking for evidence.

And here is the detail that reframes everything: **he said it to the Pharisees and the Sadducees.** Not to tax collectors, not to prostitutes, not to the man living with his father's wife. He said it to the men who came out of Jerusalem with clean records and careful hands. Hold that thought — it comes back at the end, and it is the sharpest edge in the passage.

## Confession Is Not Repentance

The difference is not subtle, and it is not academic.

| | Confession | Repentance |
|---|---|---|
| What it does | Admits the wrong | Turns from the wrong |
| What it says | "I'm sorry" | "I'm leaving" |
| Duration | A moment | A direction |
| Currency | Words | Fruit |
| Failure mode | Relief without change | Cost without theatre |
| Example | Judas — "I have sinned" (Matt. 27:4) | Peter — wept, then restored (Matt. 26:75; John 21:15–17) |

Both men failed. Both men said something true about it. One went and hanged himself; the other went back to the boat and met Christ on the shore.

Judas confessed accurately, publicly, and to the right people — and it still killed him. That is the terrifying part. **Accuracy is not the test. Direction is.**

> Confession names the problem. Repentance abandons it.

So the sermon line is not a jab at weak people. It is a description of a religious reflex: we have learned to name our sin in safety, and we have mistaken naming it for leaving it.

## What Fruit Looks Like in a Life That Was in Bondage

John's demand becomes very practical very fast. If fruit is evidence, then it is visible, and if it is visible, it can be named.

| Confession only | Fruit in keeping with repentance |
|---|---|
| "I know I shouldn't be doing this" | Telling the full truth to one trusted person |
| "I struggle with this" told to a pastor, once | Joining a recovery group and staying in it |
| Guilt after every relapse | Removing access — the app, the number, the route, the bottle |
| "God, forgive me" repeated after each episode | Changing the routine, the friendships, the places, the hour |
| Church attendance with nothing restructured | Counseling, medical help, Celebrate Recovery, a 12-step room |
| "I'll try harder" | A plan with other people in it who can see the failure |
| Hiding the fall | Confessing it fast and continuing to turn |

Jesus was not squeamish about the cost of this. "If your hand causes you to sin, cut it off" (Mark 9:43–47) is hyperbole — but the hyperbole exists to make a point that polite religion keeps trying to sand off: **radical action against what causes you to sin is not fanaticism, it is arithmetic.**

James says the same thing from another angle: "faith without works is dead" (James 2:17). Paul says it again: "Let us put aside the deeds of darkness and put on the armor of light" (Rom. 13:12). Nobody in Scripture treats a changed life as an optional accessory to a changed heart.

Now the caveat, and it matters as much as the table.

This is **not** willpower, and it is **not** a wage. The fruit does not save you; it evidences that you have been turned. A relapse is not proof that someone was never repentant — Peter denied Him three times in a courtyard and was still the rock. Repentance is a posture and a direction, and direction is measured over distance, not over a single bad night. What Scripture will not allow is a claim of turning with no trajectory at all: no honesty, no structure, no help, no change of access, year after year. That is not weakness. That is a stationary object calling itself a moving one.

## Three Kinds of Fruit

The word "fruit" is doing three different jobs in the New Testament, and confusing them wrecks the doctrine.

| | The fruit of repentance | The fruit of the Spirit | The fruit as a test |
|---|---|---|---|
| Text | Matthew 3:8 | Galatians 5:22–23 | Matthew 7:16–20 |
| Question answered | Is the turn real? | Is the root alive? | What kind of tree is this? |
| Nature | Evidence at the start | Character over a lifetime | Diagnosis, from outside |
| Grows how | Deliberate, structural | Organic, from union | Observed, not manufactured |
| Fails as | Behaviour modification | Invisibility ("I feel it, nobody sees it") | Judgment of others instead of self |

Paul's list is the famous one: love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, self-control — set deliberately against "the works of the flesh" (Gal. 5:19–21). The grammar is the point. Works are *manufactured*; fruit is *grown*. A tree does not strain to produce an apple. It is connected to something.

And the Bible keeps naming other yields of that connection, so the vocabulary is wider than Galatians 5:

- **From the Sermon on the Mount:** mercy (Matt. 5:7), purity of heart (5:8), peacemaking (5:9), love for enemies (5:44), generosity that doesn't advertise itself (6:2–4), forgiveness (6:14–15), and the plain doing of the Father's will (7:21).
- **From the letters:** faith working through love (Gal. 5:6), good works prepared beforehand (Eph. 2:10), humility that counts others more significant (Phil. 2:3–4), thankfulness (Col. 3:15–17), hospitality (Rom. 12:13; Heb. 13:2), care for widows and orphans (James 1:27), a controlled tongue (James 3:1–12 — James uses the tree image directly), perseverance under trial (James 1:2–4; 1 Pet. 1:6–7), brotherly love and good deeds (Heb. 13:16).
- **And people:** "the fruit of the righteous is a tree of life" (Prov. 11:30); Paul calls converts his fruit (Rom. 1:13; Phil. 1:22).

Here is the synthesis that made the whole conversation click for me:

> **John's fruit is the first fruit. The Spirit's fruit is the lasting fruit.**

John demands a visible break with the old life — the turn. Paul describes what grows from the root after the turn — the character. Jesus supplies the test that catches both counterfeits: false repentance (confession with no change) and false confidence (change with no root).

Which means John's fruit, on its own, has a failure mode: **white-knuckled morality.** A man can produce the behaviour of repentance for a while out of shame, fear, or pride, with nothing alive underneath. That is not a hypothetical. That is the empty house, and we are about to read it.

## Is Repentance at the Will Level?

Yes — and that is exactly as far as the yes goes.

**The will is genuinely engaged.** *Metanoia* is a change of mind that issues in a decision; the Amplified Bible's "conscious decision to turn away from sin" is not wrong. Jesus appeals to the will: "If anyone wills to do His will, he shall know" (John 7:17). The prodigal "came to himself" and said, "I will arise and go to my father" (Luke 15:17–18) — that is an act of the will, taken while he was still in the pigsty. And repentance is *commanded* (Acts 17:30), which is nonsense unless the will is addressed.

**But the will cannot carry it.** Four reasons, and they are the reason confession-only religion exists:

1. **The will is bound until it is freed.** "No one can come to Me unless the Father who sent Me draws him" (John 6:44). "Apart from Me you can do nothing" (John 15:5). Repentance is a gift as well as a command — "God granted repentance" (Acts 11:18; 2 Tim. 2:25). Whatever else it is, it is not sheer resolve.
2. **The mind has to change, not just the resolve.** *Metanoia* is literally a change of mind — a reorientation of what you love and value. That is why Paul pairs transformation with "the renewing of your mind" (Rom. 12:2). You can decide to stop something while still believing it is the thing that makes life worth living. That decision has a shelf life.
3. **The affections have to be reordered.** Repentance is not mainly "I will stop"; it is "I want something else more." Augustine's frame is the honest one: the will is moved by love. Addiction is a disordered love — a good thing, or a numbing thing, seated where God should sit. Willpower fights the desire. Repentance redirects it.
4. **The will works through means.** That is why John's fruit is concrete. The will is exercised *through* confession (James 5:16), fellowship (Heb. 10:24–25), the Word (John 17:17), and the Spirit (Gal. 5:16–17) — not in a vacuum of private determination.

So the journey is not "decide, then transform" as two disconnected stages. It is:

> will-enabled turn (repentance) → renewed mind and reordered desire (transformation) → visible fruit (proof)
> — and grace is the engine at every step, not willpower.

That is what has actually happened when someone says, "I confessed, but I never repented." The will said *sorry* and never said *leave*, and the heart was never re-aimed at anything. Confession is the doorway. Nobody lives in a doorway.

## Sin as a Ruling Power

There is a reason willpower keeps losing, and Scripture names it before we get to Matthew 12.

> "Keep back Your servant also from presumptuous sins; let them not have dominion over me." — Psalm 19:13

The Hebrew is *mashal* — to rule, to have mastery. The psalmist is not describing bad decisions. He is describing a **would-be king**. Sin is portrayed less as an act you commit and more as a power that seeks a throne in you and, once seated, commands.

Jesus treats it exactly that way:

**The strong man (Matt. 12:29; Luke 11:21–22).** A strong man, fully armed, guards his own palace, and his goods are safe — until someone stronger attacks him, overcomes him, and divides the spoil. The person in bondage is not the defendant in a debate. They are *goods in a guarded house*. Which means the rescue is not persuasion. It is an invasion by a stronger power.

**The empty house (Matt. 12:43–45).** An unclean spirit goes out, wanders, comes back, and finds the house "swept and put in order" — **and empty**. So he takes seven worse than himself. The final state is worse than the first.

Read those two together and you have the two ways recovery fails:

- **Nothing is bound:** remove nothing, confront nothing, pray a prayer, and the strong man is still seated. That is a relapse waiting for a date.
- **Cleaned but empty:** the behaviour is gone, the house is tidy, and no one has moved in. That is the more dangerous failure, because it looks like success.

> Moralism is a swept house. Repentance without occupation is a relapse with better manners.

So the requirement is not one thing but two: the strong man must be **bound**, and the house must be **filled**. Deliverance and indwelling. Otherwise you get either the old bondage back or a clean vacancy that something worse is happy to rent.

## When the Religious Need to Repent

Now go back to John's audience.

The Pharisees and Sadducees did not come to the Jordan because they were addicted, violent, or scandalous. They came with a system. And John attacked the system, not the behaviour:

> "Do not think to say to yourselves, 'We have Abraham as our father.'" — Matthew 3:9

Their confidence rested on three things: **lineage** (we are the right people), **ritual** (we are doing the right act), and **comparison** (we are not *those* people). Their sin underneath the sins was unbelief dressed as righteousness. They were not blind; they were confident.

Which means the fruit of *their* repentance would not look like "stop doing obvious sins." They thought they had. It would look like tearing down the entire framework by which they believed they were acceptable — humility, confession of need, and a turn toward the One coming after John.

That is why Jesus could say what He said:

> "Assuredly, I say to you that tax collectors and harlots enter the kingdom of God before you." — Matthew 21:31

The "sinners" had a house that needed occupying, and they knew it. The Pharisees had a house swept clean and empty, and they did not.

| | The obvious sinner | The religious person |
|---|---|---|
| The problem | Sin as a ruling power | Unbelief as a ruling power |
| The house | Occupied by the strong man | Swept clean and empty |
| The need | A stronger One to bind him | A stronger One to fill it |
| Repentance means | Turning from the behaviour and the rule behind it | Turning from self-righteousness |
| The fruit | Concrete change of direction | Humility, need, faith in the Coming One |
| The danger | Despair, and returning to the vomit | Confidence, and never knowing the vacancy |

Which gives the sharpest sentence in this whole study, and one I have had to point at myself:

> **The most dangerous sin is the settled belief that you do not need to repent.**

John did not preach fire to the drunkard and comfort to the deacon. He preached fire to the men with the best attendance record in Jerusalem.

## Honest Tensions Kept in View

1. **Fruit is evidence, never currency.** The moment "produce fruit" becomes a payment plan, we have swapped repentance for the very self-righteousness John was dismantling.
2. **Relapse is not the same as unrepentance.** Direction is judged over distance. Peter's record includes a denial and a restoration in the same week.
3. **Cleaning up is not the goal.** If sobriety were the finish line, the empty house would be a success story. It is not; it is an invitation.
4. **The Pharisee test cuts inward first.** Every time I have used "they don't really repent" about someone else, I have been doing the exact thing the sentence warns about.
5. **Fire is not ours to call down.** When a Samaritan village refused Him, James and John offered to bring fire (Luke 9:52–54). He rebuked them. The same imagery, the opposite spirit. Fire belongs to the Judge, not to the offended.
6. **Confession must be specific and embodied.** "I struggle" costs nothing. James 5:16 asks for the actual thing, said to an actual person, who will actually ask about it later.
7. **The will is real, but not sovereign.** Flatten it and you get passivity dressed as surrender. Exalt it and you get Pelagianism with a recovery plan. Both are dead ends.

## Theological Synthesis

> Repentance is the turn. Transformation is the road. The Spirit is the engine. Fruit is the proof.
>
> John demands the first fruit — a visible break with the old life, concrete enough to name.
> Paul describes the lasting fruit — character that grows because the root is alive.
> Jesus supplies the test — what a life produces reveals what it is.
>
> A tree does not strain to make fruit. It is connected to the root.
> A house does not stay clean by being swept. It stays clean by being occupied.
>
> Confession says, "I see the sin." Repentance says, "I am leaving it."
> And the strength to leave it never comes from the will that decided to.

## The Turn, as Practice

1. **Name the actual thing, to an actual person, this week.** Not the category. The thing. James 5:16 is not a mood; it is an appointment.
2. **Remove access before you need willpower.** The hand, the eye, the app, the number, the bottle, the route home. Mark 9 is drastic on purpose.
3. **Build structure, not resolve.** New routine, new hour, new room, new people. Repentance is a change of direction, and direction requires a different road.
4. **Ask for the gift, not just the effort.** "God granted repentance" (Acts 11:18). Pray for the turn, not only the strength to white-knuckle it.
5. **Aim at the affection, not just the behaviour.** Ask what the thing is paying for — comfort, numbness, control, identity — and bring that hunger to Christ instead of only fighting the symptom.
6. **Get the house filled.** Sobriety without occupation is the empty house. The goal is not a clean vacancy; it is a Resident.
7. **Judge yourself by trajectory, not by yesterday.** Get up, turn again, tell the truth about it. Repentance is a direction you keep walking, not a performance you complete.
