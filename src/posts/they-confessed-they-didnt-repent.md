A preacher said it plainly, and I wrote it down: *people confess their sins, but they do not repent.*

I did not argue with it. I have watched it be true in other people, and I have watched it be true in me. So I went back to where the phrase comes from — and found a man in camel's hair, shouting at the religious, before Jesus had preached a single public sermon.

---

<div class="thought-entry">
<span class="entry-label">Raw Thoughts</span>

**The fire came before the fire**

John is preaching wrath and fire at the Jordan — "who warned you to flee from the coming wrath?" (Matt. 3:7), "every tree that does not produce good fruit is cut down and thrown into the fire" (Matt. 3:10) — and the One he is announcing has not said anything yet. Acts 13:24 keeps the order: John preached *before* Jesus began His work.

So where did John get fire? He did not invent it. He inherited it. Isaiah 66:15–16, Malachi 4:1, Amos 7:4, Zephaniah 1:18, Daniel 7:9–10. By the first century that hope had hardened into something specific: the Messiah would arrive carrying the fire.

And then read what John actually says the Coming One will do: "He will baptize you with the Holy Spirit **and fire**" (Matt. 3:11). One baptism, two effects. Spirit first, fire after. John was not wrong about the fire. He was early. What changed is not the fire but the door.

**The verse**

> "Produce fruit in keeping with repentance." — Matthew 3:8

*Metanoia* is a change of mind that issues in a change of direction. *Karpos* is not intention; it is yield. The Amplified Bible says it without softening: "new behavior that proves a change of heart, and a conscious decision to turn away from sin."

And he said it to the Pharisees and Sadducees. Not the scandalous. The clean.

**Judas and Peter**

Both failed. Both said something true about it.

Judas: "I have sinned" (Matt. 27:4) — accurate, public, to the right people, and it killed him. Peter: wept bitterly, then went back to the boat and met Christ on the shore (John 21).

Accuracy is not the test. Direction is.

**The thing I could not get past**

Confession says, "I see the sin." Repentance says, "I am leaving it." And most of what we call repentance is the first sentence said more sincerely.

<div class="reflection-entry">
<span class="entry-label">Reflection</span>

**Why the will cannot carry it**

My first instinct was to make it a will problem — decide harder, mean it more. But the will is genuinely involved and genuinely insufficient. Jesus appeals to it ("if anyone wills to do His will," John 7:17), and the prodigal says "I will arise and go" while still in the pigsty (Luke 15:18). And yet: "No one can come to Me unless the Father draws him" (John 6:44). Repentance is commanded *and* granted (Acts 11:18).

So the journey is not "decide, then transform," as though they were two stages. It is a will-enabled turn, then a renewed mind and reordered desire, then visible fruit — with grace as the engine the whole way.

That is what has actually happened when someone confesses and never turns. The will said *sorry* and never said *leave*.

**The house**

The part that unsettled me most was Matthew 12. A spirit leaves, the house is "swept and put in order" — and empty — so seven worse move in. Moralism is a swept house. Repentance without occupation is a relapse with better manners.

Which means the danger is not only the man still in bondage. It is the man who is clean and vacant, and confident, because he has confused the two.

**Where this goes**

I worked the whole chain out properly — John's fire, the three kinds of fruit, the will, the strong man, the empty house, and why the Pharisees are the real audience of Matthew 3:8. That is now a full study: <a href="/studies/the-turn-and-the-transformation">The Turn and the Transformation</a>.

The short version, though, is this. Repentance is the turn. Transformation is the road. The Spirit is the engine. Fruit is the proof.
